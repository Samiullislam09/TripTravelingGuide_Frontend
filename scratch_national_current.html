The winter forecast for 2026-2027 depends on which forecaster you ask, and on when you ask them. Two national sources have already published theirs, two more are not out until October, and NOAA's own data on the developing El Nino is the one thread running through all of them. We checked what has actually been published so far, rather than assume all five major US winter forecasts already exist.

What is the winter forecast for 2026-2027?

A strengthening El Nino, a milder lean for much of the country, and real disagreement on the details. NOAA's data puts this El Nino on track to be historically strong. The Old Farmer's Almanac and Farmers' Almanac, both already live, agree on a milder lean but differ on where the cold and snow land.

AccuWeather and NOAA's own branded Winter Outlook are not out yet. Both are typically released in October, so this page states plainly what is confirmed right now and will be updated once the remaining two forecasts publish.

What does NOAA say about winter 2026-2027?

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center put the Nino-3.4 index at plus 1.4 degrees in its ENSO diagnostic discussion, issued 13 August 2026, and gave a greater than 90 percent chance of a very strong El Nino this fall and winter, with a real possibility of a historic event.

NOAA's routine three-month outlook maps, last updated around 20 August 2026, lean warmer than normal across the northern tier and wetter than normal across the South and Southwest. NOAA's full, branded Winter Outlook, the seasonal product with a public press briefing, is not out yet and has historically arrived the third Thursday of October.

What does the Old Farmer's Almanac predict?

A mostly mild winter with real exceptions. The 2026-2027 forecast, updated 31 August 2026, is headlined "Mostly Mild Winter, Colder, Snowier Spots Ahead," and names a colder stretch running from central New England through the Appalachians and Ohio Valley, plus Hawaii.

Its wet and dry call favors the West and parts of the South, Midwest and Florida running wetter than normal, with extra snow flagged for parts of the Northeast, the Appalachians, the West and Alaska. Editor-in-chief Carol Connare put it plainly: winter will not look the same everywhere this year.

What does the Farmers' Almanac predict?

Sharp regional contrasts rather than one clean national story. Forecaster Caleb Weatherbee's 2026-2027 forecast, last updated 3 September 2026, calls for an active, wet storm track across the South alongside quick bursts of snow and cold farther north through the winter.

The Northeast gets the least certain call of any region: a particularly unpredictable mix of rain, sleet and snow rather than a clean lean in either direction. Like the Old Farmer's Almanac, it publishes no specific snowfall figure in inches for any city.

What does AccuWeather predict for winter 2026-2027?

Nothing yet, by AccuWeather's own account. Its winter-weather hub carries no 2026-2027 seasonal forecast as of this writing, and an AccuWeather article on the developing El Nino states directly that the company's own long-range winter forecast will not release until sometime in October 2026.

What it has published so far is analysis of the same El Nino data everyone else is watching, describing this year's event as a candidate for the strongest in AccuWeather's own record. This page will add AccuWeather's actual regional forecast once it exists rather than guess at it now.

What does the Weather Channel say about winter 2026-2027?

No branded seasonal outlook here, but real, dated analysis from a named meteorologist worth reading. Jonathan Erdman's 18 August 2026 piece lays out how a super El Nino could flip the usual winter temperature pattern across the country this coming season.

A follow-up on 29 August covers how the same El Nino could affect snowfall and ski conditions specifically. Both pieces read as journalism built on NOAA's own data rather than an independent seasonal outlook product, and we treat them that way here.

Farmers' Almanac vs NOAA: how do the two methods differ?

NOAA builds its outlook from ENSO indices, ocean temperature data and statistical and dynamical climate models, all published and checkable. Both almanacs use proprietary formulas their publishers have never fully disclosed, reportedly built from some mix of sunspot activity, planetary position and historical weather patterns.

Neither approach is inherently wrong, but only one of them can be independently audited. That is a real, structural difference worth knowing before treating either source's specific regional call as equally verifiable.

How accurate has the Farmers' Almanac been?

Mixed, and noticeably better in some seasons than others. We ran a full station-by-station accuracy check against its own past regional calls, comparing what it predicted each year to what NOAA's own station data actually recorded across a run of recent winters.

That investigation found real seasons where the almanac's regional call landed close to the outcome, and others where it missed by a wide margin, roughly in line with what a pattern-based method without disclosed methodology would be expected to produce. Read it before treating any single regional call as reliable on its own.

Why do winter forecasts disagree with each other?

Because a seasonal outlook is a shift in the odds, not a promise, and different methods shift those odds differently. NOAA's El Nino data supports a milder, wetter South and a colder pocket in parts of the Northeast, and both almanacs land somewhere inside that same broad range without matching on the specifics.

Disagreement between sources is not evidence that one of them is wrong. It is what happens when several different methods all try to compress a full season into a handful of regional calls made months in advance.

What does Trip Traveling Guide's own data say?

We do not publish our own seasonal snowfall number, because no method, ours included, can responsibly predict one this far out. What we can show is the historical pattern: strong El Nino winters checked station by station have consistently landed below normal in the East and Midwest, above normal in the interior Southwest.

Chicago, New York and Pittsburgh all finished strong El Nino winters at roughly 40 to 70 percent of their own normal snowfall, while Colorado's Denver station ran above normal in the same winters. That regional split, not a single national number, is what 76 years of NOAA data actually supports.

What should you check closer to your travel date?

The National Weather Service forecast for your specific city, inside the seven-day window NOAA itself says is reliable for actual snowfall. A seasonal outlook, from any of the five sources here, is built for planning a season, not one weekend.

The limits of this comparison

Two of the five national forecasters checked here, AccuWeather and NOAA's own branded Winter Outlook, have not published yet as of this writing, and we have said so directly rather than fill the gap with a guess. We will update this page once both are out.

Neither almanac discloses its full methodology, so their accuracy cannot be audited the way NOAA's published models can, and no source here, including us, can tell you whether it will snow on one specific date. This page describes what each forecaster has actually said, not what any of them promises.
